# Networking and Referral Outreach

Referrals and direct conversations still open doors that applications alone do not. A referred candidate often gets a faster, closer look, and a short chat with someone on the team reveals what the job description leaves out. Yet most outreach fails: long messages to strangers, vague asks such as "any opportunities?", no context, no link to the role, and repeated follow-ups that turn goodwill into annoyance.

This skill plans and writes outreach that gets replies and respects people's time. It picks who to contact, researches them briefly, chooses the right ask, writes short messages for LinkedIn and email, makes referrals easy with a forwardable note, follows up once, and tracks every contact.

## Operating principles
1. **One clear ask.** Each message asks for one small, specific thing.
2. **Make it easy.** Include the job link, a two-line summary of fit and a note the contact can forward.
3. **Short and personal.** Under 120 words for most first messages, with one real reason for contacting this person.
4. **Give before taking where possible.** A useful observation, a shared connection or a kind word about their work.
5. **Respect silence.** One polite follow-up, then stop.

## Inputs
- Target roles or companies, with job links if any.
- The user's career profile or CV and LinkedIn URL.
- Existing connections: former colleagues, classmates, community members, mutual contacts.
- Preferences: tone, English variant, channels the user is comfortable using.

## When to use, and when not to
Use it for referral requests, messages to hiring managers or recruiters, informational chats, alumni or community outreach, follow-ups and thank-you notes.

Don't use it for mass messaging, buying contact lists or anything that breaks platform rules. For the LinkedIn profile itself, use `linkedin-profile`; for cover letters, use `cover-letter-writing`.

## Who to contact
| Contact | Best ask | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Former colleague or friend at the company | Referral | Warmest route; give them everything they need |
| Second-degree connection | Introduction or quick question | Ask the mutual contact first if appropriate |
| Someone in the target team | 15-minute chat about the team | Do not ask for a referral in the first message |
| Hiring manager | Short note of fit after applying | Lead with the problem you can help with |
| Recruiter | Confirm fit and eligibility, ask about process | Useful for remote and visa questions |
| Alumni or community members | Advice on the company or field | Shared context lifts reply rates |

Prioritise people whose work is close to the target role, who posted recently, or with whom the user shares a school, employer or community.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Build the target list
For each company: the role link, two to five contacts, how the user is connected, and the ask for each.

### Step 2. Research each contact briefly
Their role, time at the company, recent posts or talks, shared connections. Use one genuine detail; never fake familiarity.

### Step 3. Choose the channel
LinkedIn connection note (short, often limited to a few hundred characters), LinkedIn message, email (work email only if publicly listed) or a community platform. Use what the contact uses.

### Step 4. Write the message
Use the templates below and adjust. Keep one ask, one reason and one link.

### Step 5. Prepare the referral pack
For anyone who agrees to refer: job link and ID, the CV as PDF, and a three-line note they can forward.

### Step 6. Follow up once
If no reply after about five to seven working days, one short follow-up adding something new. Then stop.

### Step 7. Track and thank
Log every contact: name, company, date, channel, ask, reply, next step. Thank people who helped, and tell them the outcome.

## Templates
**Referral request to a former colleague**
```
Hi <name>, hope <team or project> is going well. I've seen
<company> is hiring a <role> (<link>). It lines up with my work on
<one result>. Would you be comfortable referring me? I've put a
short note and my CV below so it takes two minutes. No pressure
at all if it's not a good time.
```

**Chat request to someone on the team**
```
Hi <name>, I enjoyed your post on <topic>. I'm exploring <role type>
roles and <company>'s work on <product or problem> stands out.
Would you have 15 minutes in the next couple of weeks to tell me
what the team focuses on? Happy to work around your time zone.
```

**Note to a hiring manager after applying**
```
Hi <name>, I've just applied for the <role> (<link>). Your posting
mentions <problem>. In my current role I <result with number>.
If useful, I'd be glad to share how I approached it.
```

**Forwardable referral note**
```
<Name> is applying for <role> (job ID <id>). They have <years> years
in <field>, most recently <result>. I worked with them at <company>
and recommend a conversation.
```

**Follow-up (once)**
```
Hi <name>, a quick follow-up on my note last week. Since then
<something new: applied, a relevant article, a result>. Completely
understand if now isn't a good time.
```

## Message rules
- First line: who you are or how you are connected, in one clause.
- Second line: the specific reason for contacting this person.
- Third line: the single ask, with the time it takes.
- Last line: an easy way out, such as no pressure if now is busy.
- No attachments in a first message to a stranger; offer them instead.
- Check names, titles and company spellings twice.

## Timing and volume
Send messages on working days in the contact's time zone, ideally early in their week. A handful of well-researched messages a day beats dozens of generic ones, protects the user's account from platform limits and keeps follow-ups manageable.

## After a chat
Send a thank-you within a day that mentions one thing learned. If the chat confirmed interest, apply and tell the contact, asking whether a referral would be appropriate now. Add what you learned to the cover letter and interview notes.

## Keeping relationships warm
Networking works best before it is needed. Comment thoughtfully on contacts' posts, share useful articles and congratulate people on new roles, so the next ask comes from a relationship rather than a cold start.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| No connections at the company | Use second-degree, alumni or community routes; ask for a chat, not a referral |
| Contact says they cannot refer | Thank them; ask if they can share anything about the team |
| Referral bonus programmes | Fine to mention lightly; never pressure |
| Senior executives | Very short note; lead with a relevant result |
| Recruiter replies with a generic "apply online" | Apply, then send a brief thank-you with the application ID |
| User wants to message 50 people a day | Advise quality over volume and platform limits |

## Worked example
A user targets a fintech's Growth Lead role. They have a former colleague in the finance team, a second-degree connection in growth and an alumni contact in product. The plan asks the former colleague for a referral with a forwardable note, the growth contact for a 15-minute chat about the team, and sends the hiring manager a short note after applying that leads with an activation result. The former colleague refers within two days; the growth contact agrees to a call, which shapes the cover letter. All contacts are logged, and thank-you messages go out after the first interview.

## Common mistakes
- Asking strangers for referrals in the first message.
- Long messages with several asks.
- No job link or no summary of fit.
- Following up repeatedly.
- Not thanking people or sharing the outcome.

## Self-check before you answer
- Does each message have one clear ask and one real reason?
- Is the referral pack complete and easy to forward?
- Is there exactly one follow-up planned?
- Is every contact logged?
