# Interview Preparation

## When to use this, and when not to

Use this when someone has an interview booked, or expects one soon, and needs to prepare. It fits every common format: a recruiter phone screen, a video call, an in-person or panel interview, a technical or case round, a presentation task, and a final-stage conversation with a senior leader. It turns three inputs, the job description, the person's CV and the interview invite, into a prepared candidate: the questions most likely to come up, the evidence that answers them, research that shows the person has done their homework, and sharp questions to ask back.

Reach for a sibling skill when the job is narrower. To craft and rehearse the answers themselves, the stories, the two-minute introduction, the hard questions about weaknesses and failures, use the Interview Answers skill. For the pay conversation once an offer arrives, use the Salary Negotiation skill. To judge whether a role is worth applying for at all, use the Job Fit Evaluation skill. To build or tailor the CV the interview is based on, use the CV Writer skill.

Do not use it to sit the interview for the person, to invent experience they do not have, or to promise a result. Preparation lifts the floor under a performance; it does not guarantee an offer, and saying otherwise sets the person up to feel cheated.

## Inputs you must have before starting

Ask for anything missing before you prepare a single answer. Preparing against guesses wastes the person's evening and teaches them nothing.

- The full job description, not just the title. The wording is the richest source of likely questions.
- The company name and the exact role.
- The interview invite: date, format (phone, video, in person), expected length, and the names and job titles of the interviewers if the recruiter gave them.
- The person's CV or career profile, so every prepared answer maps to something real they have done.
- The stage: first screen, middle round, or final. Early rounds test motivation and basic fit; later rounds test depth, judgement and seniority.
- Anything the recruiter said about the shape of the interview: a presentation to deliver, a case to work, a take-home already submitted, a panel rather than one person.

If the interviewers' names are missing, say they are worth asking the recruiter for, because researching the people is one of the highest-return steps and takes ten minutes. If the format is unknown, ask; if there is still no answer, prepare for the most likely format for that stage and company, and state the assumption plainly so the person can correct it.

## Operating principles

- Prepare evidence, not scripts. A memorised script collapses under a follow-up question; a well-known example bends to whatever is asked.
- The job description is a question generator. Almost every behavioural question maps to a line in it.
- Research is worth only what the person can use in the room. Three facts they can weave into answers beat a page of notes they forget.
- Candidates judge their own performance poorly, so prepare to a standard, not to a feeling of readiness.
- Draft, never send. Thank-you notes, follow-up emails and any message to the employer are the person's to approve and send.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Read the job description as a question generator

Go through the description and pull out, in order: the core responsibilities, the named skills and tools, the seniority signals (own, lead, define, versus support, assist, help), and the business problem the role exists to solve. Each responsibility and named skill becomes a predicted question. "Owns the reporting pipeline" becomes "Tell me about a time you built or fixed a reporting process." If a requirement is repeated, or appears in the first three bullets, weight it as high probability and prepare two pieces of evidence for it, not one.

### Step 2. Research the company to a usable depth

Spend the research on what the person can actually say in the room. Gather, and write down in the person's own words: what the company sells and how it makes money; who its customers and main competitors are; one or two recent, specific items (a product launch, a funding round, a results announcement, a public post by a leader) dated within the last few months; and the company's stated values or mission in its own phrasing. Stop when you have three to five facts the person can weave into answers. A dossier they cannot recall is wasted effort. If a claimed fact cannot be verified on the company's own site or a reputable source, mark it unverified and do not let the person state it as fact.

### Step 3. Research the interviewers

For each named interviewer, find their role, how long they have been there, and their likely angle: a hiring manager probes for delivery and judgement, a future peer for collaboration, a skip-level or executive for strategy and motivation, a recruiter or HR partner for fit and logistics. Note one genuine point of common ground if there is one. Never prepare flattery; prepare relevance, so the person tailors depth and examples to who is asking.

### Step 4. Predict the format and the questions

Match the format to the stage, because a recruiter screen and a final round reward different preparation:

- Recruiter screen: motivation, salary expectations, notice period, and a walk through the CV. Keep answers short and warm.
- Hiring-manager round: behavioural questions on the core responsibilities, plus "why this role, why now".
- Technical or case round: role-specific problems; prepare the method and practice problems, not a memorised answer.
- Panel: the same ground covered from several angles; prepare to make one point to several people without repeating it word for word.
- Final or executive round: strategy, motivation, long-term fit, and the person's own questions carry more weight than earlier rounds.

Produce a ranked list: high-probability questions to prepare thoroughly, medium-probability questions to have a direction on, and two or three curveballs suited to the stage, such as "how would you improve our product?" for a later round.

### Step 5. Map evidence to questions

For every high-probability question, name the real example from the CV or career profile that answers it, and check the person can tell it with a specific result. Where no evidence exists, say so plainly: that gap is a real risk, and the honest move is to prepare the nearest true example and a candid line, not to invent one. Do not write the full answers here; that is the Interview Answers skill. This step confirms the person has something true to say to each likely question before the clock runs out.

### Step 6. Prepare the questions to ask back

Prepare five to eight questions the person can ask, because almost every interview ends with "any questions for us?", and "no" reads as no interest at the moment the interviewer forms a final impression. Tie them to the interviewer: success measures and the first ninety days for the hiring manager, daily reality and team culture for a peer, strategy and direction for an executive. Keep two in reserve in case the others are answered during the conversation. Avoid anything a quick search would answer, and hold pay and benefits for the recruiter or the offer stage, not the hiring manager.

### Step 7. Rehearse and set the logistics

Have the person say the top answers out loud and time them: most behavioural answers should run ninety seconds to two minutes. Reading silently feels like readiness and is not. Then lock the logistics: for video, test camera, microphone, link and background, and keep a phone number to hand in case the call drops; for in person, confirm the address, the route and an arrival time ten minutes early; for both, have the CV, the job description and the question list open or printed. Agree a short post-interview routine: note what was asked while it is fresh, and draft a thank-you note for the person to approve and send within a day.

## Worked example

A marketer has a second-round, 45-minute video interview for a Lifecycle Marketing Manager role at a fintech, with the hiring manager and a senior peer.

From the job description, the core responsibilities are owning the onboarding and retention email programme, running experiments, and reporting on retention. These become three high-probability behavioural questions, each needing two examples. Company research surfaces three usable facts: the company launched a new current account last quarter, it talks publicly about "activation" as its headline metric, and a competitor has just cut its free tier. The interviewers are the hiring manager, who will probe for delivery and measurement, and a senior lifecycle peer, who will probe for collaboration and craft.

The predicted set is: high probability, a retention or churn win, an experiment that failed and what changed, and how they report on lifecycle; medium, stakeholder conflict and prioritising under limited engineering time; curveball, "how would you improve our onboarding?" using the known activation metric. Evidence mapping finds strong examples for retention and reporting, a partial one for experimentation, and a gap on SMS, which is flagged so the person leads with email and is ready to say SMS is a growth area rather than claim it. Questions to ask: for the manager, what activation looks like at 30 and 90 days and how the role is measured; for the peer, how marketing and product split lifecycle ownership. Logistics: test the video set-up, keep the CV and the activation fact to hand, and draft a thank-you note to send afterwards. The output is a one-page brief the person can revise on the morning of the interview.

## Templates and prompts

Prompts the person can use with this skill:

- "Here is the job description, my CV and the interview invite. Prepare me."
- "Predict the questions for a first-round recruiter screen for this role."
- "Research this company and give me five facts I can use in the interview."
- "What should I ask the hiring manager, and what should I ask the panel?"
- "Prepare me for a final-round interview with the Head of Growth."

Interview prep brief, the output format:

```
INTERVIEW PREP: [Role] at [Company]
```

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Building a research dossier the person cannot recall. Twelve pages of notes do nothing in the room. Spot it when the research is longer than the person's attention. Instead, stop at three to five facts they can repeat from memory and work into answers.
- Preparing scripts instead of evidence. Memorised answers sound rehearsed and shatter on the first follow-up question. Spot it when an answer has a fixed wording. Instead, prepare known examples and the points to hit, not word-for-word lines.
- Preparing only successes. Interviewers push for a failure, a conflict or a weakness, and a candidate with no honest negative example looks evasive. Spot it when every prepared story is a win. Instead, prepare at least one real failure-and-learning example per round.
- Mistaking a calm feeling for readiness. Candidates judge their own performance poorly, so "I feel ready" is not evidence. Spot it when nothing has been said out loud. Instead, rehearse aloud against the predicted questions.
- Having no questions to ask. "No, I think you covered everything" reads as low interest. Spot it when the person has fewer questions than the interview can answer. Instead, prepare more than enough and keep two in reserve.

## Quality bar

Before handing over, check every item:

- Every high-probability question maps to a real example the person can actually tell, or is flagged as a gap with an honest line.
- Company facts are verified against the company's own site or a reputable source, and anything unverifiable is marked as such.
- The preparation reflects the right stage and format; a recruiter screen is not prepared like a final round.
- There are at least five questions to ask, tied to the interviewers.
- Logistics are covered: set-up or route, materials to hand, and a drafted, not sent, follow-up.
- Nothing invents experience the person does not have, and no outcome is promised.

## Edge cases and judgement calls

| Situation | What the skill does |
| --- | --- |
| Interviewers unnamed | Prepares by stage and asks the person to request names from the recruiter |
| Format unknown | Prepares for the most likely format and states the assumption |
| Very short notice | Prioritises the top three questions, three company facts and the questions to ask |
| Internal interview | Leans on known delivery and relationships, and still prepares formal answers |
| Career changer | Maps transferable evidence and prepares an honest line on the gap |
| Panel interview | Prepares to make one point to several people without repeating it word for word |
| Technical or case round | Prepares the method and practice problems, not a memorised solution |

## Tool-specific appendix

This skill is tool-agnostic and works in any agent that can read a job description and a CV. If the agent can browse the web, use it in Step 2 and Step 3 to gather current, dated facts about the company and the interviewers, and verify each against a primary source before the person relies on it. If it cannot browse, ask the person to paste the job description, the company's about page and the interviewers' profiles, and prepare from those. Keep personal data, the person's CV, their contact details and the interviewers' names, inside the session; never paste it into a third-party tool or store it elsewhere.
