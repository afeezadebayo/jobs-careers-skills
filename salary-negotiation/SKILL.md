# Salary Negotiation

## When to use this, and when not to

Use this when someone has a job offer, or expects one soon, and wants to negotiate the pay and package, or when they are preparing for a raise or promotion conversation. It researches the market rate, sets a target and a walk-away, decides what to ask for and by how much, drafts the counter, and prepares the responses to pushback.

Use the Job Fit Evaluation, Interview Preparation and Interview Answers skills for the earlier stages, and the CV Writer skill for the CV.

Do not use it to accept, sign, decline or send anything on the person's behalf, to invent a competing offer, or to promise a result. It drafts; the person decides and sends. This is not financial or legal advice, and pay-transparency and salary-history rules differ by country and state, so the person should check the local rules before acting.

## Inputs you must have before starting

Ask for anything missing before advising a number. A counter built on no market data is a guess.

- The offer in full: base, bonus, equity, sign-on, benefits, start date, title, and whether it is written or verbal.
- The role, level, location, whether it is remote, and the person's years of relevant experience.
- The person's target and walk-away (the minimum they will accept). If they do not have these, help set them first.
- Any competing offers or current pay the person chooses to share. Neither is required, and neither should be volunteered to the employer.
- The deadline the employer gave, and how the offer was delivered.

If the offer is verbal only, the first move is to get it in writing before negotiating. A large share of offers are made verbally, which lets an employer change terms without formally retracting anything.

## Operating principles

- Negotiate the whole package, not just base: bonus, equity, sign-on, start date, title, remote working and the next review date.
- Anchor on research, not a feeling. A number backed by market data holds up; a number pulled from the air does not.
- Draft, never send. Every message is the person's to approve and send.
- Asking for time is normal, and silence is a tactic to expect as well as use.
- The chance of losing an offer by countering once, politely, is far smaller than people fear.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Get the offer in writing and buy time

Thank them, ask for the full offer in writing if it is verbal, and ask for a few days to consider. A line such as "Thank you, I am really pleased. Could you send the full details in writing, and would it be alright to take a couple of days to review?" works. Do not accept or counter on the call, because a first reaction is rarely the best number. Employers withdraw offers after a polite counter far less often than candidates fear.

### Step 2. Research the market rate

Build a range from at least three sources suited to the role and country: levels.fyi and Blind for technology pay, Glassdoor, LinkedIn and PayScale more broadly, local salary surveys elsewhere, and public pay data where it exists, such as United States H-1B filings and the salary ranges now required in many United Kingdom and European Union job adverts. Record a 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentile for the role, level and location, and mark every third-party figure as an estimate. Decision rule: if the sources disagree by more than about 20 per cent, widen the search rather than trust one of them.

### Step 3. Set target, walk-away and priorities

Set three numbers before any conversation: the target (ambitious but defensible, around the 75th percentile when the evidence supports it), the walk-away (the minimum below which the person declines), and the current offer. Then decide which parts of the package matter most, because if base is capped the ask moves to sign-on, equity, start date or an early review. The walk-away is the discipline that stops the person accepting a poor deal in the moment.

### Step 4. Decide the counter

Counter above the offer, never at it. A counter of about 10 to 20 per cent above base is the common range: 10 per cent is conservative, 20 per cent is a strong position backed by solid evidence or a real competing offer. Name one specific number rather than a range, because the employer will anchor on the bottom of a range; if a range cannot be avoided, lead with the top number. Keep the figure precise and defensible rather than round and arbitrary, and never counter below the person's own target.

### Step 5. Send the counter in writing

Counter by email, not live on a call, so the case is made carefully and the person is not negotiating in real time against a professional recruiter. The email should thank them, show real enthusiasm, state the one specific ask with a single line of justification (market data, scope, experience, or a real competing offer), and open the door to a conversation. Keep it short and warm, not a legal brief.

### Step 6. Handle the response

Expect one of three replies:

- They accept: confirm, and ask for the updated offer in writing. The person may still take a day before signing.
- They ask for justification: give just enough, the market range and the scope, to let them approve the change, without reopening salary history.
- They say base is fixed: move to the other levers, a sign-on bonus, more equity, an earlier start, a better title, or a six-month review. The first counter is only about 80 per cent of the work, and a second, smaller ask often lands.

Never issue an ultimatum unless the person truly means it, and never invent a competing offer to apply pressure.

### Step 7. Close or decline

When the final numbers arrive, compare total compensation across any competing offers, not just base, and weigh base on its own as well because it sets future raises and borrowing. If the result clears the walk-away, accept, and get everything in writing before resigning anywhere. If it does not, the person can decline; a respectful "no" keeps the relationship open for the future.

## Worked example

A candidate receives a verbal offer: a base figure, a 10 per cent bonus, a four-year equity grant, and two weeks to decide. Step 1: they thank the recruiter, ask for it in writing and for three days. Step 2: three sources put the role's median above the offered base, with the 75th percentile about 18 per cent higher; each figure is logged as an estimate. Step 3: the target is set near the 75th percentile, the walk-away at their current total pay, priorities base then sign-on. Step 4: they counter 15 per cent above base, one precise number. Step 5: a short email states the ask with the market range as justification and keeps the tone warm. Step 6: the recruiter says base is near its ceiling but offers a sign-on bonus and a six-month review, and the candidate accepts the sign-on and asks for the review date in writing. Step 7: total compensation now clears the walk-away comfortably, so they accept and get the revised letter before giving notice. The figures here are illustrative, not benchmarks.

## Templates and prompts

Prompts the person can use with this skill:

- "I have an offer. Help me research the market and decide a counter."
- "Draft a counter-offer email for this offer."
- "The recruiter asked my salary expectations. What do I say?"
- "They say base is fixed. What else can I ask for?"
- "Compare these two offers on total compensation."

Counter-offer email template:

```
Subject: [Name] - [Role] offer

Hi [Recruiter],

Thank you for the offer to join [Company] as [Role]. I am excited about [specific reason].

Having reviewed it against the market for this role and level, and given [experience / scope / a real competing offer], I would like to discuss a base of [one specific number] rather than [their number]. I am flexible on how we get there and happy to look at other parts of the package too.

Could we find time to talk this week?

Best,
[Name]
```

Deflecting the salary-expectations question:

```
"I would rather understand the full role first. What range have you budgeted for this position?"
```

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Giving a number first. Naming current pay or a low expectation anchors the whole negotiation downward. Spot it when the recruiter asks early. Instead, turn the question back and ask their range.
- Countering too small. A 2 to 3 per cent counter signals the person will take almost anything. Spot it when the ask is barely above the offer. Instead, counter 10 to 20 per cent above with evidence.
- Countering with a range. The employer hears the bottom of it. Spot it when the ask is "X to Y". Instead, name one number, or lead with the top.
- Negotiating live on the phone. The person is outmatched in real time against a recruiter who does this daily. Spot it when a number is being pushed for on a call. Instead, ask for time and send the counter by email.
- Stopping after one ask, or accepting with no walk-away. People leave money and better terms on the table, or accept below their minimum in the moment. Spot it when there is no second ask and no walk-away number. Instead, set the walk-away first and look for a second, smaller ask.

## Quality bar

Before handing over, check every item:

- The market range comes from at least three sources suited to the role and country, each marked as an estimate.
- Target, walk-away and the current offer are all set before any counter.
- The counter is a single, specific, defensible number above the offer, not a range.
- The counter is drafted for the person to send, never sent for them.
- No competing offer or figure is invented, and no number is stated as fact without a source.
- The whole package is considered, not base alone.
- The response carries the not-advice note and reminds the person to check local pay-transparency and salary-history rules.

## Edge cases and judgement calls

| Situation | What the skill does |
| --- | --- |
| Verbal offer only | Gets it in writing before countering |
| Exploding offer or tight deadline | Asks for a short extension in writing and leads with the single highest-value ask |
| Entry level or first job | Counters modestly on defensible data and leans on sign-on and start date |
| Internal raise or promotion | Builds the case on scope and market and times it to a review cycle |
| Salary-history question where asking it is unlawful | Notes the person does not have to answer and redirects to the role's range |
| Equity-heavy offer | Separates base from equity, checks vesting and the strike or grant terms, and values equity conservatively |
| One offer and weak alternatives | Keeps the counter single and respectful and sets a realistic walk-away |

## Tool-specific appendix

This skill is tool-agnostic. If the agent can browse the web, gather current, dated market figures from at least three sources and label each an estimate, and verify any pay-transparency or salary-history rule against an official source for the person's location before relying on it. Keep the person's pay, offer details and personal data inside the session; never paste them into a third-party tool or store them elsewhere. The skill drafts messages for the person to review and send, and it does not contact the employer.
