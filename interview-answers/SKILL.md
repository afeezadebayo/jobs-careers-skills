# Interview Answers

## When to use this, and when not to

Use this when someone needs to answer interview questions well: the behavioural "tell me about a time" questions, the "tell me about yourself" opener, and the hard ones about weaknesses, failures, gaps and why they are leaving. It builds a bank of true stories, structures each one so the interviewer can hear exactly what the person did and what changed, and prepares the lines for the questions that trip people up.

Use the Interview Preparation skill for the step before this: researching the company and predicting which questions will come up. Use the Salary Negotiation skill for the pay conversation after an offer. Use the CV Writer skill to build the CV these stories draw on.

Do not use it to invent achievements, inflate numbers, or coach someone to mislead an interviewer. Every story must be true and traceable to something the person actually did, because fabricated detail falls apart under follow-up questions and at reference and background checks.

## Inputs you must have before starting

Ask for anything missing before writing answers. Answers built on nothing real cannot survive a follow-up.

- The person's CV or career profile. This is the raw material for every story.
- The role and the job description, so stories are chosen for relevance.
- The competencies or question themes expected, from the Interview Preparation skill, the job description, or a known format such as Amazon's Leadership Principles.
- The format and stage, which set how polished and how deep the answers need to be.
- Any specific questions the person is worried about.

With only a CV, build a first story bank and flag which competencies have thin evidence, so the person knows where they are exposed.

## Operating principles

- Every story is true. Frame for relevance, never invent.
- The Action is most of the answer. The interviewer is scoring what the person did, not the scenery.
- Say "I", not "we". A story full of "we" hides the individual contribution.
- No result, no story. End on a number or a concrete, checkable outcome.
- Prepare the detail behind each story, because the follow-up questions are where interviews are won or lost.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Build the story bank

Draw six to ten stories from the CV, covering five areas: leadership, problem-solving, collaboration, achievement, and failure or learning. Aim for at least one strong story in each area; a candidate with no failure story looks evasive. Prefer examples that are recent, relevant to the role, and measurable. One good story often answers several questions, so tag each with the competencies it covers, and note where a competency has no evidence yet, because that gap is a real risk.

### Step 2. Structure each story with STAR, weighted to the Action

Write each story as Situation, Task, Action, Result:

- Situation: one or two sentences of context. Keep it short.
- Task: one sentence on what the person was responsible for.
- Action: the bulk of the answer, in the first person, stepping through what the person decided and did.
- Result: the outcome, with a number or a concrete, checkable result, and what the person learned.

Decision rule: if the Situation and Task together run longer than the Action, cut them. The Action should be the largest part of the answer. Amazon's own interviewers expect roughly four-fifths of a behavioural answer to be "I" statements, so a story carried by "we" will score poorly however good the project was.

### Step 3. Make three lengths of each story

- Full: ninety seconds to two minutes, for "tell me about a time".
- Short: about sixty seconds, for a lighter question or a follow-up.
- One-liner: fifteen seconds, for "give me a quick example".

Most behavioural answers should land between sixty seconds and two minutes. Past two minutes, the interviewer stops listening and starts waiting for the end.

### Step 4. Write the "tell me about yourself" answer

Structure it present, past, future: what the person does now and is known for, the relevant path that led there, and why this role is the next step. Keep it to about ninety seconds. It is a headline, not a life story, and it sets up the stories that follow, so point it at the role rather than reciting the CV top to bottom.

### Step 5. Prepare the hard questions

Give a true, non-defensive answer to each likely hard question:

- Greatest weakness: a real one, with what the person does about it. Not a disguised strength such as "I work too hard".
- Why leaving, or why a gap: forward-looking and brief, never a complaint about a current employer.
- Tell me about a failure: a real failure, what was learned, and how it changed what the person does now. A humble-brag reads as dodging the question.
- Why should we hire you: two or three points tied to the role, each backed by a story.
- Salary expectations: defer politely at an early stage and handle it with the Salary Negotiation skill. Do not anchor yourself low in a screen.

### Step 6. Prepare for deep follow-ups

STAR gets a candidate through the first answer, not the drill-down. For each core story, prepare the detail interviewers push on: the numbers and how they were measured, who else was involved and what the person personally did, the alternatives considered, the trade-off made, and what the person would do differently. A single story can take ten to fifteen minutes of questioning, so a thin example that cannot be opened up is worse than a smaller one that can.

### Step 7. Rehearse and self-check

Have the person say each answer out loud and time it. Reading silently feels like readiness and is not, and candidates judge their own performance poorly, so test against real questions rather than a feeling. For each story, check the three things that decide the score: the Action is in the first person, there is a result with a number or concrete outcome, and the whole answer fits inside two minutes.

## Worked example

CV bullet: "Led a cross-functional team of eight to cut checkout drop-off."

Full STAR answer, for "tell me about a time you improved a process":

> Situation: "At an online retailer, our checkout was losing a lot of people at the payment step. Drop-off there had climbed to about 40 per cent over two quarters."
> Task: "As the product lead for checkout, I owned getting that number down."
> Action: "I pulled the funnel data and session recordings and found two causes: a slow payment page on mobile and a confusing address form. I ran five user sessions to confirm it, then prioritised two fixes over a bigger redesign the team wanted, because they were faster to ship. I wrote the spec, worked with two engineers to speed up the payment page and rebuild the address form, and set up an A/B test so we could measure the effect rather than guess. I shipped to ten per cent of traffic first, checked the numbers, then rolled out."
> Result: "Drop-off at payment fell from 40 to 28 per cent over six weeks, worth roughly 3,000 extra completed orders a month. I learned to ship the measurable fix before the big redesign."

Short version, about sixty seconds: the situation in one line, the two fixes and the A/B test, and the 40-to-28 per cent result.

One-liner: "I cut checkout drop-off from 40 to 28 per cent by fixing a slow payment page and a confusing address form, tested with an A/B rollout."

Follow-up drill to prepare for: "Why those two fixes and not the redesign?" (faster to ship, data pointed at them), "How did you know the A/B result was real?" (sample size and the six-week window), "What did the engineers do versus you?" (they built, the person specced, prioritised and ran the test), and "What would you do differently?" (test the address form change on its own to isolate its effect).

## Templates and prompts

Prompts the person can use with this skill:

- "Turn my CV into a bank of STAR stories."
- "Here is a behavioural question. Give me a STAR answer from my experience."
- "Write my ninety-second 'tell me about yourself' for this role."
- "Help me give a truthful answer to 'what is your greatest weakness'."
- "Drill me with follow-up questions on this story."

STAR story template:

```
STORY: [short name]   Covers: [competencies]
Situation: [1-2 sentences of context]
Task: [1 sentence: what I owned]
Action: [the bulk, first person: what I decided and did]
Result: [number or concrete outcome + what I learned]
Follow-up notes: [metrics and how measured | who did what | alternatives | trade-off | what I would change]
```

"Tell me about yourself" template:

```
Present: I am a [role] who [what you are known for, one proof point].
Past: I got here by [the relevant path, kept short].
Future: I am looking for [why this role, tied to the company].
```

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Answering in "we". The interviewer cannot tell what the person did. Spot it when the story has no "I". Instead, rewrite the Action in the first person and name the person's own decisions.
- No result, or no number. A story with no outcome is an anecdote, not evidence. Spot it when the answer ends on "and that went well". Instead, add the measured result and what was learned.
- A long Situation and a short Action. The context eats the time and the interviewer never hears the work. Spot it when the setup is longer than the doing. Instead, cut the setup to one or two lines.
- Memorised and brittle. A word-for-word answer collapses on the first follow-up. Spot it when the person cannot answer "why did you do that?". Instead, learn the story and its details, not a script.
- Inventing or inflating numbers. Fabricated detail fails under drill-down and at background checks, and it is dishonest. Spot it when a number cannot be sourced. Instead, use the real figure, or describe the outcome without a number.

## Quality bar

Before handing over, check every item:

- Every story is true and traceable to something the person actually did.
- The Action is the largest part of each answer and is told in the first person.
- Every story ends on a result, with a number where one exists.
- Each core story has follow-up detail prepared: metrics, who did what, alternatives, trade-off, and what to change.
- There is at least one true failure story and a real, non-disguised weakness.
- No answer runs past two minutes in the full version, and the "tell me about yourself" is about ninety seconds.
- Nothing invents or inflates achievements.

## Edge cases and judgement calls

| Situation | What the skill does |
| --- | --- |
| Thin evidence for a competency | Uses the nearest true example and flags the gap, rather than inventing one |
| Early-career or student | Draws on study, volunteering and part-time work, as the STAR method allows |
| Career changer | Frames transferable actions and results, without claiming a title or skill not held |
| Confidential numbers | Describes the result in relative terms ("cut drop-off by about a third") instead of a banned figure |
| Leadership Principles format | Maps stories to the principles and leads with customer impact |
| A genuine failure with no clean recovery | Keeps it true: the failure, the lesson, and the changed behaviour, not a forced happy ending |

## Tool-specific appendix

This skill is tool-agnostic and works in any agent that can read a CV. If the agent can hold a back-and-forth, use it to run a mock drill: ask a question, take the answer, then push with the follow-ups in Step 6, because the practice that helps most is the follow-up, not the first answer. Keep the person's CV and personal details inside the session; never paste them into a third-party tool or store them elsewhere. The skill writes answers for the person to deliver; it does not speak for them in a live interview.
