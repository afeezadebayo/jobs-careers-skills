# Remote and International Jobs

A remote job advert rarely means "anyone, anywhere". Most are limited to countries where the employer can legally pay people, a band of time zones, or regions such as EMEA. Many candidates apply to roles they can never be hired for, miss roles they could take through an employer of record (EOR) or contractor setup, accept contractor rates that are really pay cuts, or fall for remote-job scams. Moving abroad adds another layer: visa routes, sponsorship and relocation support.

This skill helps people find and win remote and international work they are eligible for. It decodes eligibility wording, sorts roles into eligible, worth asking or not eligible, explains the hiring setups that make cross-border work possible, compares pay fairly across employee and contractor offers, flags tax and contract questions to check with professionals, reviews visa sponsorship options and screens out scams.

## Operating principles
1. **Eligibility first.** Check whether the employer can hire the user where they live before any CV is written.
2. **Read the setup, not just the word "remote".** Employee, EOR, contractor or relocation each change pay, tax and rights.
3. **Ask early.** One clear question to the recruiter can save weeks.
4. **Compare like with like.** Convert contractor rates and employee salaries to the same basis before deciding.
5. **Not legal or tax advice.** Rules change and differ by country; point to official sources and professionals for decisions.

## Inputs
- Where the user lives, their citizenship and any existing work rights.
- Target roles, seniority and pay expectations, in their currency.
- Setups they accept: remote employee, EOR, contractor, relocation, hybrid abroad.
- Time zone and the hours they can overlap with other regions.
- Job postings or companies to assess.

## When to use, and when not to
Use it for remote job searches across borders, checking whether a remote role is open to the user, EOR or contractor offers, comparing international offers, visa sponsorship questions and relocation decisions.

Don't use it as legal, immigration or tax advice; those decisions need official sources or qualified professionals. For daily searching, use `daily-job-opportunities`; for writing applications, use `cv-writer` and `cover-letter-writing`.

## Decoding eligibility wording
| Posting says | Usually means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| "Remote (US)" or "US only" | Must live in and be authorised to work in the US | Not eligible unless already authorised; ask only if the company also lists EOR hiring |
| "Remote, EMEA" or "Europe" | A set of countries in the region, often not all | Check the country list; ask if the user's country is unclear |
| "Remote, anywhere" or "worldwide" | Usually via contractor or EOR setups | Eligible; confirm the setup |
| "UTC-1 to UTC+3" or "4 hours overlap with CET" | Time zone requirement rather than country | Eligible if the user can meet the hours |
| "Must have the right to work in X" | No sponsorship | Not eligible without existing rights |
| "Visa sponsorship available" or "relocation support" | Employer will support a move | Eligible for relocation; check the route |
| No location stated | Unknown | Ask before applying |

Countries are often restricted because the employer needs a legal entity or EOR to run payroll, benefits and tax there, or because of labour law, data and security rules, or sanctions and export controls.

## Hiring setups
| Setup | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Local entity employee | Employer has a company in the user's country | Normal employment rights; local pay bands |
| Employer of record | A provider such as Deel, Remote, Oyster or similar legally employs the user locally on the company's behalf | Benefits vary by country; contract is with the EOR |
| Independent contractor | User invoices the company | No paid leave, pension or health cover unless built into the rate; user handles tax |
| Relocation with visa | Employer sponsors a work visa and often helps with the move | Visa timelines, ties to the employer, family rules |
| Hybrid abroad | Must live near an office in another country | Usually needs a visa or existing rights |

## Workflow

### Step 1. Record the user's situation
Residence, citizenship, existing work rights, accepted setups, time zone overlap and pay floor.

### Step 2. Assess each role
Read the full posting and careers pages for country lists, time zones, setup and sponsorship. Classify it:
- **Eligible:** clearly open to the user's country or time zone, or worldwide.
- **Ask first:** region wording is vague, or the company hires through EOR elsewhere.
- **Not eligible:** restricted to countries where the user has no work rights and no EOR or sponsorship is offered.

### Step 3. Ask the recruiter early
One short message before investing in a tailored CV:
```
Hi <n>, before I apply for <role>: I live in <country> (<time zone>)
and can overlap <hours> with your team. Do you hire in <country>,
for example through an employer of record or as a contractor?
```

### Step 4. Compare pay fairly
Put offers on the same basis: annual, same currency, before tax, and including what the setup provides.

| Item | Employee or EOR | Contractor |
|---|---|---|
| Base pay | Salary | Rate x billable days |
| Paid leave and holidays | Included | Unpaid; deduct from billable days |
| Pension, health cover | Often included | User pays |
| Employer taxes and social contributions | Paid by employer or EOR | Often the user's responsibility |
| Equipment, internet, accounting | Sometimes provided | User pays |

As a rough guide, contractor rates often need to be well above an equivalent salary to match it once leave, benefits and taxes are counted. Check the user's own numbers rather than using a fixed multiplier.

Also note whether the employer pays location-based bands or one global band, and use salary research for the hiring region, not only the user's home market.

### Step 5. Flag tax, payment and contract questions
List questions for a tax adviser or accountant: tax residency, how foreign income is taxed, registration for contractors, and record keeping. Note practical payment questions: currency, bank transfer or payment platform, fees, exchange rates and local foreign-exchange rules. For contracts, check notice periods, termination terms, intellectual property clauses, payment terms and currency, equipment and expenses, and which country's law applies.

### Step 6. Review relocation and visa options (when relevant)
Identify whether the employer sponsors visas and which route might apply, then point the user to the official government source. Examples to check: the UK register of licensed sponsors and Skilled Worker route, the EU Blue Card, Canadian employer-driven and Express Entry routes, and US employer-sponsored categories. Requirements, salary thresholds and quotas change often, so never state them from memory.

### Step 7. Screen for scams
Remote roles attract fraud. Drop or flag roles that:
- Ask the user to buy equipment, software or training.
- Send a cheque or crypto and ask for part of it back.
- Interview only by chat app, with no video call or named staff.
- Offer very high pay for vague tasks, or hire without interviews.
- Use a company domain that does not match the email address.

### Step 8. Prepare the application
Pass eligible roles to `cv-writer` with the setup and time zone details. Advise a short line in the letter or form when time zones matter, such as "I work on UTC+1 and can overlap four hours with London". Follow the user's preference on showing city and phone for roles abroad.

### Step 9. Prepare for remote ways of working
Check the basics a remote employer may ask about: reliable internet with a backup connection, backup power where outages are common, a quiet workspace, a suitable laptop and habits for asynchronous work across time zones.

## Output format
```
1. User situation summary (one paragraph)
2. Role-by-role eligibility table: role | wording | verdict | setup | next step
3. Recruiter question template(s) for "ask first" roles
4. Pay comparison on the same basis (if offers exist)
5. Questions for a tax adviser and contract points to check
6. Visa or relocation notes with official links (if relevant)
7. Scam flags
```

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| User has dual citizenship | Check eligibility under each citizenship |
| Company says "global" but lists countries elsewhere | Trust the specific list and ask |
| Contractor offer far below local salaries | Show the like-for-like comparison before the user decides |
| Employer wants the user to travel to an office often | Check visa rules for business travel |
| Sanctioned or restricted country | Explain that many employers cannot hire there and suggest legitimate alternatives |
| User considering misstating location | Advise against it; it breaks contracts and can end employment |

## Worked example
A data analyst living in a West African country is shortlisted for three remote roles. The first says "Remote, US" with no EOR mention, so it is not eligible. The second says "Remote, EMEA" and lists eligible countries on its careers page, which include the analyst's country, so it is eligible. The third says "worldwide, contractor" and offers a day rate. The skill drafts a recruiter question for a fourth, vague posting, converts the contractor rate to an annual figure after unpaid leave and self-paid health cover, shows it is below the EMEA salary offer, and lists questions for an accountant about contractor tax registration.

## Common mistakes
- Applying to country-restricted roles without asking.
- Treating contractor day rates as equal to salaries.
- Ignoring time zone requirements.
- Relying on old visa rules or salary thresholds.
- Paying anything to get a job.

## Self-check before you answer
- Is every role classified as eligible, ask first or not eligible, with a reason?
- Are pay figures compared on the same basis?
- Are tax, legal and visa points framed as questions for official sources or professionals?
- Were scam signs checked?
